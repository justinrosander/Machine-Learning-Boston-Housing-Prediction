EDA Findings Narrative: Boston Housing Price Prediction
Date: October 3, 2026

This analysis began with a practical objective: understand what drives variation in house prices (MEDV) and prepare for a robust linear-regression workflow. The dataset provided 506 neighborhood-level observations with complete coverage across all variables, and earlier quality checks on the processed data found no missing values, no duplicate rows, and no critical integrity issues in engineered features. That gave us a reliable baseline for interpretation before modeling.

The univariate analysis set the tone for the rest of the story. MEDV was not symmetric; it was positively skewed (skewness = 1.108), with the mean (22.533) higher than the median (21.2). Most neighborhoods clustered in moderate price ranges (Q1 = 17.025, Q3 = 25), with a thinner tail toward high prices and an upper cap at 50. This shape suggested that a log transformation of the target could improve model behavior. Several predictors showed similarly non-normal behavior: CRIM had a heavy right tail, ZN contained many zeros with occasional high values, and LSTAT also showed right skew. These patterns justified selective transformation rather than blanket scaling alone.

Bivariate analysis then clarified the strongest direct relationships with price. Two features stood out immediately: LSTAT had a strong negative correlation with MEDV (-0.738), while RM had a strong positive relationship (0.695, near the strong-correlation threshold). In practical terms, neighborhoods with higher lower-status population percentages tended to have lower median house values, while neighborhoods with more rooms tended to have higher values. Additional variables such as PTRATIO, INDUS, TAX, and NOX showed moderate negative associations with MEDV, reinforcing that social, infrastructure, and environmental factors all move with price.

Looking across predictors (not just predictor-to-target), we observed clear multicollinearity structure. The strongest pair was RAD-TAX (0.910), followed by NOX-DIS (-0.769), INDUS-NOX (0.764), AGE-DIS (-0.748), and several other high-correlation pairs. This mattered because it signaled overlapping information across urbanization and accessibility variables. In other words, multiple columns were describing related underlying neighborhood conditions, which can destabilize raw linear coefficients if left unmanaged.

The multivariate diagnostics deepened that finding. The workflow expanded from pairwise correlation to model-space checks: VIF diagnostics for beyond-pairwise collinearity, added-variable plots for marginal contribution after controlling other predictors, and residual diagnostics to detect structure that simple scatterplots can miss. Cross-validated model metrics were included as stability checks, and standardized coefficients were visualized to compare directional influence while holding other variables constant. These were used descriptively and diagnostically, not as formal hypothesis tests.

Outlier analysis was handled in a targeted way. Instead of removing rows aggressively, candidate predictor outliers were flagged by IQR burden and treated with capping/winsorization where necessary. This preserved sample size while reducing the leverage of extreme values. Complementary multivariate outlier screening (Mahalanobis distance) helped identify observations that were unusual in combined predictor space, giving a second lens beyond univariate boxplots.

Taken together, the narrative is coherent: house price variation is strongly linked to socioeconomic and housing-composition signals (especially LSTAT and RM), while a dense network of correlated urban/environmental predictors requires careful regularization or feature selection. The data is sufficiently clean and informative for meaningful descriptive and predictive analysis, and the preprocessing choices (target transformation, selective outlier treatment, collinearity diagnostics, and standardized model preparation) position the project for defensible downstream modeling.

In short, the EDA journey moved from shape and quality checks, to pairwise signal detection, to multivariate structure diagnostics—building a consistent evidence trail from raw distributions to model-ready insight.
